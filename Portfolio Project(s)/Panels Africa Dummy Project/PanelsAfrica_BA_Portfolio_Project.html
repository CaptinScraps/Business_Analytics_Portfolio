Project Overview

Background
Panels Africa (Pty) Ltd is a South African retailer specialising in manga, manhwa, manhua, and Western comics. Founded in 2019, the business operates an online storefront, a Johannesburg walk-in store, and sells through third-party marketplaces including Takealot and Bob Shop. The product catalogue spans over 400 SKUs across genres such as Shōnen, Seinen, Isekai, and Horror, covering both physical volumes and digital bundles.

Over the past year, Panels Africa has accumulated 1 000 rows of transactional sales data covering orders, returns, customer segments, genre performance, pricing, and fulfilment channels. That data currently lives in a spreadsheet. There is no dashboard, no standard reporting process, and no formal way for the merchandising team or management to interrogate trends. Decisions are made on instinct rather than evidence.
The Problem
Sales are growing but margin is shrinking. Management cannot identify which genres, channels, or customer segments are most profitable. Stockouts on Shōnen titles are frequent while Seinen inventory sits unsold. No one has a clear view of return rates by genre or fulfilment method.
The Proposed Solution
Build a Business Intelligence and Reporting Platform — a structured data pipeline, a cleaned and modelled dataset, and an interactive dashboard — that allows management, the merchandising team, and finance to answer business questions from a single source of truth.
Timeline & Budget
Duration: 4 months (16 sprints × 1 week)
Budget: R180 000 total (tools, dev, BA time)
Target Go-Live: End of Q3 2025
Methodology: Agile (Scrum)
Core Constraints
Must integrate with the existing WooCommerce storefront and Takealot order export. No enterprise data warehouse budget. Output must be accessible to non-technical staff. POPIA compliance required for any customer-level data.

Stakeholder Register

Identify everyone who will be impacted by, or can influence, the Panels Africa Analytics Project. Group them by internal and external, assess their interests and influence level, and plan how you will engage each one throughout the project.

📖 How to Identify Stakeholders

Start by asking the right questions for each category:

Internal Stakeholders (inside Panels Africa)
  • Who is funding or sponsoring this project?
  • Who will build, configure, or maintain the platform?
  • Who will use the dashboards and reports on a regular basis?
  • Who manages the data the platform will consume (WooCommerce, Takealot exports, spreadsheets)?
  • Who is responsible for POPIA compliance and data governance?
External Stakeholders (outside the organisation)
  • Are there marketplace partners (Takealot, Bob Shop) whose data feeds the platform?
  • Are there third-party tool vendors whose systems will be integrated?
  • Are customers whose data will be processed considered stakeholders from a POPIA perspective?
💡

Remember: a stakeholder doesn't have to log into the platform to be affected by it. Store staff who rely on stock decisions informed by the data are stakeholders. Finance who approve the budget are stakeholders. Think broadly first; you can always narrow down.

Step 2 of 20 · Phase 1: Planning & Requirements

Scope & Boundaries

Define exactly what this project will deliver — and, just as importantly, what it will not deliver. A clear scope document prevents scope creep, manages expectations, and gives the development team a clear mandate.

📖 How to Define Scope

Scope is a contract between the business and the project team. It answers: what problem are we solving, by what means, and what are we not going to do?

In-Scope: ask yourself what must be true for this project to be considered a success
  • What data sources must be ingested and cleaned?
  • What reporting views or dashboard pages must be built?
  • What integrations are required for the data to flow automatically?
  • Which user types must be able to access and use the output?
Out-of-Scope: ask yourself what could be requested but must be deferred
  • Are predictive models or AI recommendations in scope for v1?
  • Is a customer-facing portal in scope?
  • Will the platform include accounting system integration (e.g. Xero)?
Assumptions: things you are treating as true without formal confirmation
  • What format will the source data arrive in?
  • What tooling budget has been approved?
  • Who will maintain the platform after handover?

Step 3 of 20 · Phase 1: Planning & Requirements

Risks & Dependencies

Identify what could go wrong, what the project depends on that it cannot control, and how you plan to respond to each. A risk log is a living document — it evolves as the project progresses.

📖 Risk vs Dependency — What's the Difference?

  • Risk: an uncertain event that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on the project. Most project risks are negative. You score them by likelihood and impact.
  • Dependency: something the project must have in place before a particular piece of work can proceed. Dependencies can be internal (another team must complete X first) or external (a vendor must provide Y).
For each risk, assess:
  • Likelihood — how probable is this event? (High / Medium / Low)
  • Impact — if it happens, how bad is it? (High / Medium / Low)
  • Mitigation — what can you do now to reduce likelihood or impact?
  • Owner — who is responsible for monitoring and responding?

Epics & Features

Break down the Panels Africa Analytics Project into major functional areas (Epics) and the specific capabilities within each one (Features). This is the structural skeleton that your user stories and backlog will be built from.

📖 Epics vs Features vs User Stories

  • Epic: a large body of work that represents a major functional area of the product. Too big to be built in a single sprint. Example: "Sales Analytics"
  • Feature: a specific capability within an epic. Smaller than an epic; may take 1–3 sprints. Example: "Genre Performance View"
  • User Story: the smallest unit of deliverable value, written from the user's perspective with acceptance criteria. Fits within a single sprint. Example: "As a Merchandising Manager, I want to filter the genre view by date range so I can compare this quarter to last quarter."

At this stage, you are working at the Epic and Feature level only. User stories come next in Step 5.

Step 5 of 20 · Phase 1: Planning & Requirements

User Stories & Requirement Types

Write sprint-ready user stories for the Panels Africa Analytics Project. Every story needs a persona, a goal, a reason, and acceptance criteria that can be tested. Also identify which stories represent functional requirements and which are non-functional.

📖 The User Story Formula

As a [persona], I want to [action/goal] so that [business value / reason].

Every story must also have Acceptance Criteria (AC) — testable conditions that must be true for the story to be considered done. Write these as "Given / When / Then" statements or as a numbered checklist.

Functional vs Non-Functional Requirements
  • Functional (FR): what the system must do. "The system must display genre revenue as a bar chart filterable by date range."
  • Non-Functional (NFR): how the system must perform. "The dashboard must load within 4 seconds on a standard office connection."

NFRs are often forgotten but they define quality. Common NFR categories: Performance, Security, Accessibility, Reliability, Maintainability, POPIA Compliance.

Traceability Matrix

Link every requirement to its source (business objective), the epic it belongs to, the user story that captures it, and the test case that will validate it. The traceability matrix is the thread that runs through the entire project — it proves nothing falls through the gaps.

📖 What Goes in a Traceability Matrix

  • Requirement ID: the unique reference (e.g. US-001)
  • Business Objective: the strategic goal this requirement serves (e.g. "improve margin visibility")
  • Epic: E1, E2, etc.
  • Feature: F2.1 etc.
  • Test Case ID: the QA/UAT test that validates this requirement is met
  • Status: Not Started / In Development / In Testing / Done

Step 7 of 20 · Phase 1: Planning & Requirements

Backlog Prioritization

Score and rank every user story using MoSCoW (Must Have / Should Have / Could Have / Won't Have this time). The output is a sprint-ready prioritized backlog — the team picks from the top of this list each sprint.

📖 MoSCoW Definitions

  • Must Have: non-negotiable; the platform fails without it. Can it launch without this? If no, it's a Must.
  • Should Have: important, high value, but there is a workaround. It will be in v1 if time allows.
  • Could Have: nice to have; low impact if left out. Goes to v2 unless there is spare capacity.
  • Won't Have (this time): explicitly deferred. Documenting Won't Haves manages expectations and prevents scope creep.

Step 8 of 20 · Phase 1: Planning & Requirements

Definition of Ready & Done

Set the non-negotiable entry criteria (Definition of Ready) and exit criteria (Definition of Done) for stories on the Panels Africa project. These two definitions align the whole team on quality and process standards before a single sprint begins.

📖 DoR vs DoD

  • Definition of Ready (DoR): the checklist a story must pass before it can be pulled into a sprint. It prevents teams from starting work on stories that are underspecified.
  • Definition of Done (DoD): the checklist a story must satisfy before it can be called complete. It prevents "done-ish" stories from accumulating technical and quality debt.
